GPS is reliable until it isn't — cold drains batteries fast at altitude, signal drops in deep valleys, and screens crack. It's why every guide on our Everest Base Camp and Kilimanjaro trips still carries a printed topographic map as a non-negotiable piece of kit, regardless of how many satellites are overhead.

## A map teaches you the shape of a place

There's a real difference between following a blue dot and reading contour lines. A paper map forces you to understand a valley's shape, where the climbs are, where water sources sit — context a phone screen tends to flatten into "turn left in 200m." Travelers who glance at a paper map before a trekking day consistently report feeling more oriented on the trail, not less.

## The three-fold test

Before any multi-day trip, fold your route map into quarters and check you can navigate using just the section showing. If a torn or lost page would strand you, that's worth solving before departure, not during it — pack a second copy or a laminated version for anything genuinely remote.

> A phone can fail silently. A paper map fails visibly — and that difference matters more than people expect until the moment it doesn't.

## Where we still hand out paper maps

On the Landmannalaugar trek and the Discovery Islands kayaking route especially, both remote enough that a dead battery is a real inconvenience rather than an abstraction, every traveler gets a printed route overview at the trip briefing. Consider it standard kit, not a novelty.
